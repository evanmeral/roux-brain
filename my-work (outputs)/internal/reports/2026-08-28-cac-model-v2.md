### 2. Margin: 55% is wrong and isn't uniform ✅ correct — and it's worse than 55%

⚠️ **Open question for Robert:** Shopify's "cost per item" is fully populated (Evan confirmed), but
**is it landed cost — tariffs and inbound freight included — or raw product cost?** Robert

### 3. "20% net profit" is really contribution margin ✅ correct, and the most important point
This is the one that changes the numbers. Overhead — payroll, rent, agencies, software — has to be
covered before anything is profit. Setting the CAC ceiling at full contribution means every acquired
order contributes exactly zero to overhead.

**Related, and worth flagging to Jay:** Jay's stated preference was *"$2M at 20% over $3M at 8%"* —
that 20% is a **net** margin. Contribution and net are different numbers, and any target should say
which one it means.

# What ROUX would add to Robert's approach

**1. Robert's order-level framing is better than ROUX's unit-level — and more generous where it counts.**
He asks for net revenue **per order**, not per unit. That's right: you acquire an *order*, not a unit.
It also captures attachment. A Cooling Ring lists at $55.99 but nets ~$80 an order — ring buyers add
things. A 120 QT lists $715–805 but nets ~$850. Unit-level economics undercount both.

**2. His read on accessories is correct, and the numbers are stark.**
A ~$21 ceiling on a Cooling Ring order means no standalone paid acquisition works. As add-ons and
retargeting — where the acquisition cost is already paid — they're excellent. Cooling Ring is the
**highest-margin item in the catalogue at 54%**, so it should be attached to everything and advertised
alone to nobody.

**3. One addition: campaigns don't sell one product.**
`BPM_TOF_Manual` moves a mix, so a single-product ceiling doesn't apply. Two options — either hold
mixed campaigns to a **weighted ceiling** based on their actual product mix (computable from Shopify),
or restructure so campaigns map to product lines. **ROUX recommends restructuring**: it's what makes
per-product ceilings enforceable rather than theoretical, and it's the natural next step after Robert's table exists.

**4. Overhead allocation method matters.**
Allocating overhead as a flat % of revenue is simple but penalises high-ticket items — a 120 QT
carries 6× the overhead of a fryer for roughly the same handling. Worth Robert and Jay deciding
whether to allocate by **revenue share** or **per order**. Per-order allocation would raise the
accessory ceiling and lower the commercial one.
